The Rail Switchers

Evergreen Created 18 Aug 2026 Last tended 8 Oct 2026

Every few months, the headlines return. Financial commentators declare that BRICS is building a unified currency to dethrone the US dollar.

It makes for great drama. It is also fundamentally wrong.

Building a single monetary union across China, India, Russia, Brazil, and South Africa is a macroeconomic fantasy. Their capital controls are too mismatched. Their foreign policy goals diverge. Their monetary frameworks do not fit into a single box.

The ministers and central bankers meeting in New Delhi and Moscow know this. They are not trying to launch a joint fiat currency.

They are doing something far more practical: they are changing the underlying payment rails.

The Manhattan detour

To understand why BRICS wants to switch rails, look at how cross-border trade works today.

Suppose a buyer in New Delhi purchases goods from a supplier in São Paulo. The buyer holds Indian Rupees (INR). The seller wants Brazilian Real (BRL).

In a rational network, money would move directly from New Delhi to São Paulo. It doesn't. Instead, the payment takes a mandatory detour through Manhattan.

[Buyer in India (INR)]
       │
 (Local Debiting)
       ▼
[Indian Issuing Bank]
       │  (SWIFT Message - ISO 20022)
       ▼
[US Correspondent Bank (Nostro Account in USD)]
       │  (Fedwire / CHIPS Settlement in NY)
       ▼
[US Clearing Bank (USD Intermediate Pair)]
       │  (SWIFT Message)
       ▼
[Brazilian Correspondent Bank (Vostro Account in USD)]
       │  (Local FX Conversion: USD -> BRL)
       ▼
[Brazilian Acquiring Bank]
       │  (Local Crediting)
       ▼
[Supplier in Brazil (BRL)]

Total Latency: 24 to 72 Hours  |  Friction: Multi-tier fees, opaque FX spreads

Here is the step-by-step reality of that transaction:

  • The Message: The Indian bank sends an ISO 20022 message over SWIFT.
  • The Conversion: The Indian bank converts rupees into US dollars at a Nostro account in New York.
  • The Settlement: The dollars clear through Fedwire or CHIPS.
  • The Final Hop: The dollars land in Brazil, where a local bank converts them into Real.

This detour takes 1 to 3 days. It charges fees at every hop. Most importantly, because the money cleared through a US bank, the transaction falls under US legal jurisdiction.

For 50 years, this was the only way to settle global trade at scale. That monopoly is ending.

Two engineering paths

At the FIBAC 2026 conference in Mumbai, Reserve Bank of India Governor Sanjay Malhotra laid out the ground truth.

There is no common BRICS infrastructure yet approved, and no joint currency on the table. Instead, BRICS members are discussing two distinct engineering paths to connect existing national systems:

  1. Fast Payment System (FPS) Bridges: Connecting domestic instant payment networks — like India's UPI and Brazil's Pix — via direct API gateways.
  2. Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) Interoperability: Linking national CBDCs to settle sovereign trade directly on digital ledgers.

Domestic fast payment systems have transformed local economies. India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) handles over 13 billion transactions a month. Brazil's Pix dominates daily commerce across Latin America.

Connecting these systems does not require replacing national currencies. It requires API translation and bilateral messaging protocols.

Wholesale settlement: multi-CBDC ledgers

Retail payment bridges work well for small transfers. Large-scale commodity trade — oil, wheat, raw minerals — requires wholesale sovereign settlement.

This is where multi-CBDC platforms like Project mBridge enter the picture.

Developed by the BIS Innovation Hub alongside central banks in China, the UAE, Thailand, and Saudi Arabia, mBridge uses a dedicated, permissioned distributed ledger.

[Commercial Bank A (China)] ──────────────────────────────► [Commercial Bank B (UAE)]
       │                                                            │
       │  (1. Issue e-CNY on mBridge Ledger)                        │  (4. Redeem e-AED)
       ▼                                                            ▼
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                            mBridge DLT Ledger                                │
│                                                                              │
│  * Central Bank Nodes validate state updates.                                │
│  * Atomic Swap Execution: Payment-versus-Payment (PvP).                      │
│  * e-CNY directly exchanged for e-AED on-chain without USD routing.          │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Instead of keeping Nostro accounts in foreign commercial banks, participating entities hold sovereign digital currencies directly on a shared ledger.

Settlement executes via Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) atomic swaps:

  • Bank A transfers digital Yuan (e-CNY).
  • Bank B transfers digital Dirhams (e-AED).
  • The smart contract executes both transfers simultaneously.

Both legs settle at the exact same instant. There is no settlement delay, no counterparty credit risk during transit, and no dollar intermediary.

The three frictions of direct pairs

It is easy to draw architecture diagrams. It is hard to run global liquidity networks. Bypassing correspondent banks introduces three severe engineering and market-structure problems:

It is easy to connect two servers with an API. It is much harder to trade two currencies nobody wants to swap directly.

According to data from the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), the US dollar sits on one side of roughly 89% of all foreign exchange transactions globally. It does this because it acts as a vehicle currency — the universal liquid buffer.

Think of it like a barter market.

Suppose a buyer in New Delhi wants to exchange Indian Rupees (INR) directly for Brazilian Real (BRL).

In today's market, almost no commercial bank keeps massive, active order books for INR/BRL. Finding an entity in São Paulo who holds Real and wants Rupees at the exact second you want to trade is rare.

Because there is almost no organic market depth (a deep pool of active buyers and sellers), attempting a direct trade exposes you to three severe problems:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     THE THREE FRICTIONS OF DIRECT PAIRS                 │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Wide Spreads: Middlemen charge higher fees to take on rare currency. │
│ 2. Slippage: Large trades drain available supply, driving up cost        │
│    mid-trade.                                                           │
│ 3. Volatility Risk: Without USD as a shock absorber, exchange rates      │
│    jump.                                                                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  1. Wide Bid-Ask Spreads: Because direct INR/BRL trades are rare, the market makers who facilitate them charge a massive middleman fee (a wide spread) to cover their risk.
  2. Slippage: If an Indian importer tries to buy $10 million worth of goods directly in Real, the order will swallow the available INR/BRL liquidity instantly. The exchange rate will “slip” rapidly against the buyer midway through the transaction, making the last million far more expensive than the first.
  3. The USD Buffer: When those same trades run through the dollar (INR -> USD -> BRL), they leverage deep global markets where trillions move daily. Competition between banks keeps the spread paper-thin.

If BRICS wants to bypass the dollar in real-time payments, member central banks cannot just build payment switches. They must become market makers of last resort — holding pools of each other's currencies or funding active central bank currency swaps to absorb that slippage themselves.

Direct payment bridges rely heavily on pre-funding. To settle real-time payments between India and Brazil, an Indian bank must keep foreign currency sitting idle in a Brazilian account. This traps huge amounts of capital globally.

CBDCs solve pre-funding via atomic swaps, but an atomic swap still requires a market maker willing to hold the incoming foreign currency on its balance sheet.

BRICS is not a political monolith. India and Brazil operate within Western financial channels and must maintain strict international anti-money laundering (AML) and FATF standards. Russia operates under severe international sanctions.

Building a single, interconnected payment rail across nations with fundamentally conflicting compliance laws creates massive technical edge cases.

Key Takeaway: The friction in global payments is no longer about messaging speed — it is about foreign exchange liquidity and regulatory compliance across sovereign borders.

Fragmentation, not disappearance

The claim that the dollar will vanish overnight is false. The dollar remains the primary global unit of account and store of value.

However, in payments infrastructure, monopoly is giving way to fragmentation.

[Old World: Monolithic Rail]
Global Trade ──► SWIFT + USD Clearing Banks ──► Settlement

[New World: Multi-Rail Web]
               ┌──► Local Fast Payment Bridges (UPI / Pix)
Global Trade ──┼──► Multi-CBDC Ledgers (mBridge)
               ├──► Regional Networks (CIPS / RuPay)
               └──► SWIFT / USD Clearing (Legacy High-Value)

We are moving away from a single global highway toward a web of interconnected regional toll roads.

The future belongs to payment orchestration platforms — the gateways, APIs, and smart sub-ledgers that can dynamically route payments across SWIFT, instant payment bridges, and CBDC networks based on cost, speed, and regulatory path.

BRICS isn't building a new currency. They are building the switches that bypass the old guards.
