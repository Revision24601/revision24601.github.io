12 Years Later — BRICS 2.0

Evergreen Created 8 Oct 2026 Last tended 8 Oct 2026

What I got wrong and what I got right.

In August 2014, during a summer school at the University of Exeter, I wrote an essay for Professor David Boughey called “BRICS 2.0 — Looking past the BRIC wall”. The argument was simple: the original BRIC economies were slowing, the acronym had stopped describing where the growth would come from, and anyone looking for the next decade of emerging-market returns should look at Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey, South Korea and the Philippines instead.

Twelve years is a long enough half-life for those claims to be graded. This page is the grading. One rule before I start: demographic potential is not the same thing as growth. In 2014 I treated a young, large population as if it were itself an engine. The last decade taught me it is at best the fuel — and fuel only does something in an engine somebody has actually built. That mistake runs through the whole essay, and it is worth tracing exactly where it paid off and where it didn't.

What 2014 me actually claimed

Stripped of undergraduate padding, the essay made five testable claims:

  1. The BRICs were slowing. China had fallen from an average of 11% growth to under 8%; Brazil and Russia were commodity plays that had taken “the wind out” of their sails when Chinese demand cooled.
  2. The BRICs no longer formed “a rising economic axis” — inherent tensions were complicating “anything practical,” so the bloc's promise was fading.
  3. Location is a first-order variable. Being on an axis connecting two regions — Mexico between the US and Latin America, Turkey between Europe, Russia and the Arab world — would convert geography into trade and investment flows.
  4. Demographics is the screen. Large populations with a rising share of workers relative to dependents would set the contenders apart from an aging China and Russia — and if Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria and Turkey “got their act together,” Chinese-style double-digit growth was on the table.
  5. Each contender had a differentiator. Mexico was taking the lead from China in manufactured exports as Chinese labour costs rose. Indonesia had Southeast Asia's largest middle class. Nigeria had youth, scale and room to run. Turkey had location and entrepreneurial depth. The Philippines had call centres and remittances. South Korea, I admitted, might already be “an emerging market of 20 years ago.”

You'll notice the criteria are the classic 2014 emerging-market shopping list: big, young, stable-ish, well-located. What the list conspicuously does not include is the thing that turned out to matter most — whether the country had a functioning export engine and a currency its own citizens believed in. Keep that in mind; it decides most of the grades below.

The scorecard

Slower, yes. Weaker, no. Right, with a sting. I predicted deceleration and implicitly read it as decline.

The corrective came hard — a 50% policy rate and inflation back down to roughly 30% by the end of 2025 — while exporters kept shipping through all of it. Half right, violently. The geography thesis survived its own stress test; the “stable economy” assumption did not.
The Philippines' differentiators were its call-centre industry and “massive international remittances” Both engines simply compounded. Remittances hit a record $38.34 billion in 2024 — about 8.3% of GDP — while the BPO sector reached $38 billion of revenue and 1.82 million jobs. Right. The least glamorous pick aged the most gracefully.
South Korea “could be considered… an emerging market of 20 years ago” It graduated, exactly as suspected, and never needed the acronym. Right (and my most self-aware sentence of 2014).
If the contenders “get their act together,” Chinese-style double-digit growth may follow Nobody got there. No contender sustained double digits, and the decade's biggest growth stories were driven by trade position, industrial policy and services exports — not by median age. Wrong, and it's the flaw in the original framework, not just one pick.

What I got right

The slowdown call. The essay's premise was that the old BRIC story was ending, and on China it was simply correct: the 11% era gave way to a managed step-down that eventually settled around 5%. I also flagged the mechanism that hurt Brazil and Russia most — their dependence on selling raw materials into Chinese demand — and that is basically how the next decade played out for both.

The location thesis. This was the most durable argument in the essay, and Mexico is the proof. I claimed Mexico had “taken the lead from China in manufactured exports” and that its geography between two blocs was a structural advantage. By 2023, Mexico had displaced China as the United States' number-one trade partner, and the driver is the one 2014 me named: proximity plus rising Chinese costs. Turkey stress-tested the same thesis from the other direction — the essay called its location a “double-edged sword” two years before a spillover refugee crisis and a coup attempt made that phrase read almost literal.

The Philippines. I picked the archipelago for two unglamorous reasons: call centres and remittances. Neither one spiked; both compounded. A record $38.34 billion in remittances in 2024 and a $38 billion BPO industry employing 1.82 million people is what a real differentiator looks like after twelve years of compounding — an engine, not a projection.

The bloc-scepticism, partly. The essay noted that “inherent tensions complicated the task of getting anything practical done.” Twelve years on there is still no common BRICS currency and the New Development Bank remains a modest operation. The bloc never became an economic union. It just never faded the way I said it would.

What I got wrong

Nigeria, root and branch. The 2014 paragraph is a catalogue of the era's optimism: Africa's biggest player, median age under 18, third most populous country incoming, GDP heading to $5 trillion by 2050. What actually happened was a fall in GDP per capita from about $3,201 in 2014 to roughly $1,621 in 2023, inflation around 33% in 2024, and a decade Nigeria's own business press calls lost. I even listed the risks — “corruption to theft of natural resources” — and then weighted the demographics more heavily anyway. The lesson writes itself: a young population is a labour supply, not a growth rate. Without a currency people trust and something the world wants to buy, scale just means more people sharing a smaller pie.

The fade-out that wasn't. My single worst structural call was “the BRICs no longer look like a rising economic axis.” Economically, member growth diverged wildly — that part held. But the political brand didn't fade; it expanded. Four new full members in 2024, Indonesia making ten in January 2025, a partner-country tier underneath. And the punchline I could not have written in 2014 without laughing: one of the countries on my “what comes after BRICS” list joined BRICS, and another (Nigeria) queued up as a partner. The demand for an alternative axis wasn't dying. It was reorganizing.

Double-digit demography. The essay repeated the era's claim that the contenders might “match Chinese-style double-digit growth rates.” None did. China's run had specific ingredients — export industrialization, infrastructure at scale, a deliberately cheap currency, and a state willing to aim all of it — and I treated rapid growth as something demographics grants instead of something policy extracts. Indonesia, my most “potent consumer market” pick, grew a respectable 5%-ish for a decade and is nowhere near the income-per-head path the 2014 projections implied. That is fine, healthy growth. It just isn't the chart I graded against.

The blind spot: the bloc became plumbing

Here is the part of the retrospective nobody could have assigned me in 2014, because it took my current job to see it. I graded “BRICS 2.0” as a contest between groups of economies. The thing that actually changed since 2014 is not which countries are in which acronym — it is that several of them stopped waiting for a summit to matter and built alternative payment infrastructure instead. Brazil's Pix and India's UPI are domestic fast-payment systems at a scale that changes how money moves; The Rail Switchers traces the bridges being built between them and the wholesale multi-CBDC experiments running in parallel.

In other words: the “2.0” I was looking for in trade blocs turned up in settlement rails. The original BRICs didn't fade, and their challengers didn't replace them — both generations ended up cooperating on the plumbing layer instead, which is the layer a payments engineer now gets paid to think about. 2014 me wrote about money. 2026 me works where it lands.

What I'd tell 2014 me

  • Your best variable was geography, not demography. Weight it first.
  • Ask of every contender: what does it sell the world, and do its own citizens hold its currency voluntarily? Mexico and the Philippines could answer both. Nigeria couldn't, and the demographic chart didn't save it.
  • “Slowing” is not “declining.” China decelerated into dominance of the industries everyone else now wants.
  • Acronyms are marketing with an index attached. The real units of analysis are trade position, monetary credibility, and infrastructure — including the financial kind.
  • Keep the scepticism about summits producing anything practical. Then watch what the central banks build between summits.

The same exercise, run forward: 2038

Twelve years was the distance between the 2014 essay and this grade, so let twelve years be the unit. If a 2038 version of me is going to mark homework, the honest thing to do is hand him claims written the way I now know claims should be written: specific, checkable, and built on engines rather than labels. Here is what we should all be thinking about for 2038 — five questions, each with its grading criterion attached.

  1. Does the plumbing ever carry real weight? The expanded BRICS' practical work is payment rails. By 2038 we'll know whether bridges between fast-payment systems and multi-CBDC settlement graduated from pilots to volumes that matter — or whether dollar liquidity quietly won again. Grade it on measured cross-border volumes settled off the correspondent-banking path, not on summit communiqués.
  2. Who holds the Mexico seat next? The 2014–26 winner wasn't the youngest country; it was the one parked beside a giant economy with a sellable factory base. Vietnam is running that exact play now for the China-plus-one supply chain. If geography-plus-one-engine is the durable pattern, 2038's “best call” won't be in anyone's current acronym — it'll be the neighbour nobody bothered to initial.
  3. India is the demographic-bet country now. Everything I wrongly expected demography to do for Nigeria is now being forecast for India, with one difference: it already has engines (services exports, UPI-class rails, manufacturing incentives). The 2038 grade is income per head, not GDP headlines. Population was never the scoreboard — it's what each person produces and keeps.
  4. Nigeria gets re-graded. The post-2023 reforms — subsidy removal, exchange-rate unification — are the “get their act together” clause from 2014, finally being tested. Forget the $5 trillion by 2050 line. The only marker that matters: does GDP per capita clear its own 2014 level, and stay cleared? Until then the demographic chart is a promise, not a plan.
  5. Which label survives? BRICS outlived its obituary by expanding; “Global South” is the vaguer label now doing the same work. My 2038 bet: the acronyms persist as diplomatic brands, but the returns — the actual money — accrue to whoever owns the rails, corridors and chokepoints underneath the labels. If that's wrong, it'll be because a bloc finally built institutions as real as its summits.

And the discipline that matters more than any single call: write the claim down, with the number that would prove it wrong, and leave the receipts where a future you can't edit them. Twelve years is long enough for the world to mark your homework honestly — if you kept the answers.

What I'm watching now

Three open threads, in garden spirit: whether BRICS expansion stays a diplomatic brand or keeps converting into payment plumbing; whether Indonesia as a full member changes Southeast Asia's centre of gravity; and whether Nigeria's post-2023 reforms — subsidy removal, exchange-rate unification — can restart the economy its demographic chart always promised. Mexico and the Philippines, the boring compounders of this story, I'll keep tending quietly. They're the part of the 2014 essay I no longer have to apologize for.
